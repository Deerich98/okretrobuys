# What Makes A Game Lot Worth More (And What Doesn't Matter)

When people send us a photo of a game lot, the apology usually arrives with it. "The boxes are rough." "There's sticker residue on some." "I don't think we have all the cables." Here's the thing: half of what people apologize for barely moves the number, and half of what they never mention is exactly what we're squinting at the photo trying to find. So let's even the information out.

What actually moves a lot's value

Complete beats mint — and complete means the paper. A game with its case, its artwork, and its manual is a different object from a loose disc, and the manual is the piece most likely to have wandered off decades ago. That drawer of loose booklets somewhere in your house? Those reunite with their games and upgrade both. Same for cardboard inserts and the little registration cards nobody ever mailed — the boring paper is quietly the rare part.

The accessories nobody thinks about. Memory cards. Off-brand controllers with the weird ergonomics. Link cables, adapters, the light gun that only worked on the old TV. Accessories didn't survive at the same rate as consoles, which makes the bag of them worth genuinely more than it looks like. If it's rattling in the bottom of the tub, it counts.

Still plugged in beats boxed away. Counterintuitive but true: the console still wired up behind the TV cabinet — dusty, in service until recently — is often a better sign than one boxed in the attic. Attics and garages in Oklahoma summers are hard on electronics; the living room is climate control. Either way we want to see it, but don't assume the one still hooked up is the worthless one.

The oddballs. Every lot has its strange passenger: the imported game, the demo disc, the rental-store case with the plastic sleeve, the console variant in a color you forgot existed. Don't pre-filter these out because they seem like clutter. The strange passenger is sometimes the headline.

What doesn't matter (much)

Sticker residue and marker names. A childhood name written on a cartridge in permanent marker is practically a category standard. It matters far less than people fear.

Dust and grime. Cleaning is our job, not yours. A dirty lot photographed honestly is more useful to us than a scrubbed lot where somebody's cleaning method did more harm than the dirt.

You not knowing if it works. Don't test anything, seriously. Don't go buy a weird cable to find out. Condition unknowns are baked into how we look at lots, and testing is work we're set up to do anyway.

The games "everybody had." Yes, some titles were printed in the millions and the internet will tell you they're worth little individually. In a lot, they're still volume, and volume still counts. Don't pull them out; the lot sells as a lot.

The point of all this

You don't have to do any of the sorting, reuniting, testing, or valuing yourself — that's literally what we're for. One photo of everything as it sits, and we'll come back with a real cash number for the whole thing. OKC metro, we pick up or meet.

But now you know what we're looking at when we look: the paper, the accessories, the passengers. Which means you also know why we always say the same thing — don't tidy it first. The stuff you'd tidy away is half the value.
