# You Read The Manual In The Car On The Way Home

You had it in your hands before the car left the parking lot. Not the game — you couldn't do anything with the game yet. The manual.

And you read the whole thing before you got home.

Cover to cover before the driveway

It's a strange thing to explain to anyone who came up after it. The manual was not a troubleshooting document you consulted when something went wrong. It was the first chapter of the experience, and it was designed that way.

There was a story section. Two or three pages of setup written in a slightly overheated voice — a kingdom in peril, a corporation gone wrong, a rival racing team. Names of characters you hadn't met. A map of a world you hadn't seen, with regions labeled in a font that felt important.

There was a controls diagram. FIG. 1 — the controller, drawn in clean line art with labels pointing to each button, and a table telling you what each one did in each state.

There were the character bios, which everybody read twice and which nobody needed.

By the time the car pulled in you knew the plot, the cast, and the button layout of a game you had not yet played for one second. You were, in the most literal sense, prepared.

The parts nobody skipped

Every manual had them:

The health and safety page. Read once, ignored forever, and now oddly moving — a serious warning about sitting too close to the screen, printed for a kid who was going to sit directly in front of it for six hours.

The blank NOTES pages at the back. Which absolutely got used: passwords, level codes, maps drawn in pencil, the name of the friend who beat the level you couldn't.

The warranty card, which was meant to be mailed and never was, and which is now sometimes the most collectible piece of paper in the whole package.

The insert for other games — a small glossy sheet advertising three titles from the same publisher. It was an advertisement. You studied it like a catalogue.

Why the manuals still matter

Here's the practical part, and it's the reason we bring this up at all.

A complete game is worth meaningfully more than a loose one, and "complete" usually means the case, the manual, and any inserts. The manual is the piece most likely to be missing, because it's paper, it's small, and it separates from everything else the moment somebody tidies up.

Which means the drawer of loose manuals a lot of Oklahoma City households still have — no cases, no discs, just the booklets — is not junk paper. Those booklets are exactly what the people with loose games are looking for.

If you kept them, that instinct was right.

The drawer, one photo

If there's a stack of manuals somewhere in your house — in a drawer, in a shoebox, in the bottom of a tub with the cables — take one photo of the stack.

You don't need to identify them or match them to anything. Send the picture and we'll tell you what's there, and come back with a real cash number.

And tell us: did you keep yours, or did they vanish sometime around the third move? Both answers are extremely common.
