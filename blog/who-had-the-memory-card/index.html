Who Had the Memory Card

August 6, 2026

# Who Had the Memory Card

Whoever owned the memory card ran the sleepover. That was just the arrangement.

It didn't matter whose house it was, or whose console it was, or who was better at the game. If you brought the card with the saves on it, you had leverage, and everybody in the room knew it. You picked the game. You picked the file. And if you decided it was time to go home, an entire evening's progress went home in your pocket.

The economics of a small grey rectangle

Memory cards were the strangest piece of hardware in the house, because they were the only part that held anything of yours.

The console was a console. The controllers were interchangeable. The games could be borrowed. But the card had the file — 40 hours of something, the unlocked characters, the good save right before the hard part — and that made it disproportionately powerful for a thing the size of a matchbox.

Which is why:

Everybody wrote their name on theirs. Masking tape, a sticker, nail polish, a marker line down the side. Cards were identical and universally portable, which meant they went missing constantly.

Blocks were currency. You had a finite number and games were greedy about them. There was always one game hogging fifteen blocks that you were never going to play again, and deleting it was a whole decision.

There was always one slot nobody touched. Somebody's file, from something, that you were fairly sure was important. You worked around it for years rather than find out.

The incident

Everyone reading this either deleted somebody's save or had theirs deleted. There is no third category.

The classic version: you needed blocks, you were in the file menu, the file was named something unhelpful like "AAA" or "1", and you made a judgement call at ten at night that you were not qualified to make. Sometimes it was your brother's. Sometimes it was your own, and you'd forgotten what it was.

The other classic version: someone saved over your file because there was one slot and they wanted to play too. No malice, total devastation.

There was no undo. That was the thing. Everything about that era of gaming was permanent in a way that nothing is now — no cloud backup, no restore point, no support ticket. It was just gone, and you either started again or you didn't.

Where the cards are now

Here's the odd part: the consoles got sold or thrown out, the games got traded in, but the memory cards are almost always still in the house.

They're small enough that they never got a proper decision made about them. They ended up in the drawer with the cables, or in the bottom of a plastic tub, or in a shoebox at a parent's place with a couple of controllers and a power brick. They didn't get discarded — they got filed, permanently, in the place things go when nobody wants to deal with them.

If you've got a drawer like that, it's usually got more in it than you remember. Cards, controllers, adapters, cables, the odd loose cartridge, and occasionally something genuinely uncommon that got put in there because it was small.

That drawer is worth a photograph.

So — whose card was it?

Tell us in the comments: whose card ran your house, and who deleted a save they shouldn't have? We've heard some good ones, and there is apparently no statute of limitations on this particular grievance.
