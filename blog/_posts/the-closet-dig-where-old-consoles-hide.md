Old consoles don't get thrown away. They get *put somewhere* — and then the somewhere becomes permanent.

Nobody decides to keep a Nintendo for twenty years. What happens is that it gets unplugged during a move, goes in a box marked "electronics," the box goes in the closet, and the closet does the rest. If you've got ten minutes, here's where to actually look.

## The six places it's hiding

**The hall closet, top shelf.** Above the coats, behind the extra blankets. This is the number one spot, because it's the shelf nobody reaches into except when something has to be put away *out of the way.*

**The garage shelf, in a lidded tub.** Usually mixed with holiday decorations. If the tub has a lid and hasn't been opened in years, it's worth the ten seconds.

**Under the bed in the guest room.** Specifically the guest room, because that's where the previous occupant's life got stored when the room changed jobs.

**The bedroom closet floor, in a shoebox.** Loose cartridges and controllers end up here. So do memory cards and the good link cable.

**The box that moved twice and never got opened.** You know the one. It's still taped. It's been in three houses.

**Your parents' house.** Genuinely — a huge share of what we buy is somebody's childhood setup that stayed behind when they moved out and has been in the same closet since.

## Grab the boring stuff too

When you find the console, don't stop at the console.

The **power supply and AV cables** matter — the correct original cords for older systems are frequently harder to find than the machine itself. **Controllers**, especially original first-party ones. **Memory cards.** The **manuals and cases** in the drawer nearby. Odd **accessories** — adapters, multitaps, light guns, chargers, screen covers.

And take the whole tangle in the bottom of the box. What looks like a nest of dead wires to you is often the part that completes a set.

## Does it matter if it doesn't work?

Not as much as you'd think. Send it anyway.

Plenty of what we buy powers on fine after a cleaning, and plenty of it is worth something even when it doesn't. Yellowed, dusty, missing a cord, one broken controller — all still worth showing us. The only thing that genuinely kills value is water damage, and even then, ask.
