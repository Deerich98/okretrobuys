Pull the cartridge. Blow into the bottom like you're playing a harmonica. Slam it back in. Press reset.

It is, without much competition, the most widely practiced piece of folk tech support in human history. Nobody taught it to you. Nobody had to. Somewhere around 1988 it entered the groundwater and every kid in America knew it.

Here's the part that ruins it slightly: it probably wasn't the blowing.

## What was actually going wrong

A cartridge is a circuit board with a row of exposed metal contacts along the bottom edge. The console has matching pins that press against them. That's the whole interface — no lasers, no moving parts, just metal touching metal.

Two things break that connection over time. **Oxidation:** exposed metal contacts dull and film over from years of air and fingers. **Wear:** the connector inside the console is a spring-loaded slot, and thousands of insertions relax those springs so they press less firmly.

Either one gives you the same symptom — a flashing screen, a scrambled title, or a solid color where the game should be.

## So why did blowing "work"?

Mostly because of what you did *around* the blowing.

You pulled the cartridge out and put it back in. That reseating alone re-scrapes the contacts against the pins and re-seats the connection, and it's the step that actually fixed things a large share of the time. The blowing was the ritual wrapped around the fix.

Moisture may have helped a little in the moment — a thin film of condensation on a dull contact can improve conductivity temporarily. Emphasis on temporarily.

## The bad news

Your breath is warm, humid, and carries small amounts of saliva. Depositing that on exposed metal contacts is, over years, exactly the recipe for corrosion — the thing that caused the problem in the first place.

So the ritual that fixed it today made it worse next year. Which is a very 90s outcome.

What actually cleans a cartridge is a cotton swab with isopropyl alcohol, gently, until the swab stops coming away gray. It works remarkably well on carts that seem dead. It is also, admittedly, nowhere near as satisfying.

## But we're not going to stop

None of this matters. If you grew up with a cartridge console, you will blow into one until the day you die, and so will we. It's not a repair technique at this point — it's a greeting.

Be honest in the comments though: **was it the blowing, or was it pulling it out and putting it back in?**
