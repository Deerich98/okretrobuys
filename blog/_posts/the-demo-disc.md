# The Demo Disc Was Its Own Kind Of Christmas

It came taped to the front of a magazine, and it was never the reason you bought the
magazine. It was just *there* — a paper sleeve with a disc inside, stuck to the cover with
two strips of tape that always took a bit of the cover with them.

And it kept you busy for a month.

## Three levels, four hundred times

The genius of the demo disc was accidental. Nobody set out to make a piece of media you'd
replay obsessively; they set out to advertise. But the constraint did the work.

You got one level. Maybe two. Sometimes a timed slice that cut you off mid-jump with a
screen telling you where to buy the full version, which you could not do, because you were
nine and it was February.

So you played the level again. And then again, differently. You found the edges of it — the
wall you could stand on, the enemy that didn't respawn, the shortcut that saved four
seconds. You knew that level better than you know most rooms in your own house.

People who had the full game later would say the demo section felt small. It never felt
small.

## The sampler disc problem

Some discs had a menu. Six or seven demos on one disc, which sounds like abundance and was
actually a ranking exercise.

There was always one you loved, two that were fine, and three you clicked into exactly once
— usually a racing game with one track, or a sports title where you didn't know the
controls and lost 12–0 to the computer before quietly returning to the menu.

And there was often a video section. Trailers, developer interviews, a montage set to a
song you didn't recognize but somehow still know. Nobody watched those on purpose the first
time. Everybody watched them eventually, because the disc was what you had.

## Why the discs stayed in the drawer

Here's the part that matters now: almost nobody threw them out.

They weren't games, exactly, so they didn't get traded in. They weren't worthless, so they
didn't get binned. They ended up in the drawer under the TV, or in the sleeve of a CD wallet
between two burned discs with nothing written on them.

Which means a surprising number of Oklahoma City households still have them, sitting in a
box in a closet, sandwiched between the manuals and the third-party controller nobody liked.

Some of those discs are genuinely sought after now — certain kiosk and press discs never had
a retail release at all, and the people who collect them have very specific gaps to fill.
Most are common. All of them are the kind of thing that's easier to send a photo of than to
research.
