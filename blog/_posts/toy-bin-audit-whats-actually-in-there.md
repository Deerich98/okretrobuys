# The Toy Bin Audit: What's Actually In There

Every garage in the metro has one: the tub. Maybe two tubs. Lid slightly domed from overpacking, label long peeled off, contents last inventoried never. The kids aged out, the bin got filled, and it's been holding down that corner of the garage through at least two summers you meant to deal with it.

Here's what we can tell you from opening a lot of other people's tubs: you probably don't know what's in yours. Not really.

## The layer system

Toy tubs stratify like archaeology. The top layer is whatever went in last — usually the biggest, lightest stuff, and honestly, usually not the good layer. The middle is where the everyday toys live: the vehicles missing a wheel, the playsets missing their people, the action figures in whatever pose they froze in a decade ago.

The bottom is where it gets interesting. Small, heavy things sink. Loose accessories — the tiny weapons, the trailer hitch, the capes and helmets that make a figure complete — work their way down through years of shuffling and settle at the bottom like sediment. So do the odd, dense little items nobody remembers: the figures that came from a fast-food promotion that turned out to matter, the small diecast pieces, the parts of things.

That bottom layer is why we say don't sort. Sorting the tub is how the pieces get separated from the things they belong to — and a figure reunited with its accessories is worth more than both halves apart. The person doing the sorting almost never knows which little gray part goes with which figure. We often do. That's the whole job.

## Beat-up rides along

The other thing people get wrong about the tub: apologizing for condition. "It's all pretty played-with" — of course it is. They were toys. Played-with is the normal state of the category, and a tub with real play wear can still hold plenty worth buying, especially when the loose parts are all still swimming around in there together. The beat-up stuff rides along with the good stuff; that's how lots work. We'd rather see an honest tub than a curated one.

## The five-minute audit

You don't need to inventory anything. Here's the whole process: drag the tub somewhere with light, take the lid off, and take one photo from above — close enough that the mess is visible. That's it. Don't stage it, don't dig, don't pull out the "good" stuff and line it up. The mess *is* the information.
