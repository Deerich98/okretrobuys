# August in the OKC Garage

There is a two-week window in Oklahoma City every year where nobody in their right mind opens the garage door for anything but the car.

That window is roughly now.

By early August the metro has been baking for weeks, and an attached garage with a west-facing door is comfortably twenty degrees hotter than the yard. Step in at three in the afternoon and it hits you like an oven door. Whatever project you had planned in there gets moved to "when it cools off," which around here means late October.

## The stuff that survives the summer untouched

Every OKC garage has the same geological layers, and August is when they go dormant:

**The far wall.** Whatever went in first and got pushed back. Usually the deepest and most interesting stratum.

**The tubs from the last move.** Taped, labelled optimistically, opened never. If you moved in an August you already know they've been through this heat several times.

**The shelf above the workbench.** Where small things go to be safe, which functionally means where small things go to be forgotten.

**The corner by the water heater.** Reliably the worst place to store anything and reliably where the games and toys ended up.

If any of that has games, consoles or toys in it, this is genuinely the time of year to think about it — not because the heat is doing them any favours, but because it isn't.

## What Oklahoma summers actually do to this stuff

Not scaremongering, just what we see:

**Heat and plastic don't get along.** Cartridge shells and console cases warp and discolour with repeated heat cycling. Light-coloured plastic yellows faster and doesn't come back.

**Cardboard and humidity are worse.** Boxes soften, corners round, printing lifts. A boxed item in a hot garage loses value in a way the same item in a hall closet doesn't.

**Batteries eventually leak.** Anything with a battery compartment — handhelds, remotes, battery-backed cartridges — is on a clock. A leak takes an item from "worth money" to "worth nothing" in one step, and it's the single most common way we see a good piece ruined.

**Mice and wasps like garages.** They especially like cardboard boxes full of soft materials.

None of this is urgent-today urgent. It's just why the tub that's been out there for six summers is usually in worse shape than the identical tub that spent those summers in a spare bedroom.

## While you're out there

If you're going to be in the garage anyway, two quick things worth doing regardless of whether you ever sell anything:

1. **Pull the batteries out of anything that has them.** Five minutes, and it's the single highest-value preventative act available to you.
2. **Move any boxed items inside.** Even a hall closet is dramatically better than a garage in an Oklahoma August, and boxes are where the value concentrates.
