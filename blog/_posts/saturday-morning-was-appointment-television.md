# Saturday Morning Was Appointment Television

You got up earlier on Saturday than you did for school. Nobody made you. Nobody had to.

There was a window — roughly seven to eleven, depending on the channel and the year — and if you missed it, you missed it. There was no catch-up, no on-demand, no "it'll be on later." The whole week funnelled into one morning, and you were on the couch for it with a bowl in your lap before anybody else in the house was awake.

## The rules of the morning

**You were quiet on purpose.** Getting up early was fine. Waking a parent up while doing it was a strategic error that could cost you the whole morning. There was an art to the volume knob.

**The bowl was part of it.** Whatever the cereal was — and everybody has a specific one — it was eaten in front of the TV, and the milk at the bottom was drunk from the bowl. This was not negotiable and no one has ever done it any other way.

**You knew the running order.** Not from a guide. From memory. You knew which show was at 8:00 and which one you'd sit through to get to the one at 8:30, and you knew exactly how long the ad breaks were.

**The ads were part of the show.** Toy commercials on Saturday morning were doing the same job the catalogue did in the autumn — telling you precisely what you wanted, at a volume and frame rate no reasonable child could resist. Half the toys in the closet started as a thirty-second spot between cartoons.

**And then it just stopped.** Around eleven the block ended and something for adults came on, and that was the signal that the good part of the weekend was over. You went outside.

## What actually made it good

It's tempting to say the shows were better. Some were. Plenty weren't, honestly — a fair few of them were an extended toy advert with a plot attached, and we all knew it even then.

What made it good was the **scarcity**. One morning a week. If your family had one television, you also had to *negotiate* for it, which meant getting up first was a competitive advantage. And because everyone your age was watching the same four hours of the same shows, Monday at school was a shared conversation. You didn't have to explain what you'd watched.

That's the part that's genuinely gone. Not the cartoons — most of them are a search away. The *appointment*. Nothing is at 8:00 any more; everything is available always, which is better in every way except the one that made Saturday feel like an event.

## Where it all ended up

The console that lived under that television is usually still in the house somewhere. So is the controller, and the games, and often a shoebox of accessories that came with it.

They tend to migrate: living room, to a bedroom, to a closet, to the garage, to a tub. Each move puts them one step further from being used and one step closer to being forgotten. Most of the collections we look at made exactly that journey, and the owner is always a bit surprised to find the whole setup is still together.

If yours took that route too, it's worth a photograph. Some of it's worth real money and some of it isn't, and the only way to know is to look.
