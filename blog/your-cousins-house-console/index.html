# You Were Never Going To Win At Your Cousin's House

There was one house in the family where the good console lived, and it was not your house.

It was your cousin's. Or the friend down the street whose older brother had a job. Either way, the arrangement was the same: you went there, you played, and you lost.

The math was never in your favor

Look at it honestly and it was a rigged contest from the start.

He had the console every day. You had it for four hours at Thanksgiving.

He had every game — including the one you'd only read about in a magazine — and had already found the shortcut, the glitch, and the character everybody agreed was cheap.

He had the good controller. You had the other one. The one with the sticky button, or the D-pad that registered "down" as a suggestion, or the cord that had been stepped on so many times you had to hold it at a particular angle.

You were playing a different game than he was, on worse equipment, once a year.

The rules of somebody else's house

Every one of these will be familiar:

He picked the game. It was his house. That was simply the constitution.

He was player one. Also constitutional. Player two was a lesser office and everyone knew it.

There was a rule you'd never heard of. Announced mid-match, always in his favor, always retroactive.

The screen-watching accusation went in exactly one direction, and it was never at him.

And the reset button was his. The single most powerful weapon in the house. A match could be ended by executive action at any moment, and it usually was, at the exact moment you got ahead.

Losing at his house is why you know the games at all

Here's the thing worth saying, twenty-odd years later: that house is where you learned the hobby.

You didn't own any of it. You had no control over any of it. And you can still, right now, name the games on that shelf in order, describe the room, and remember which corner the console sat in and how the cables ran.

That's what those objects do. They don't store their value in the plastic — they store it in where you were the first time you saw them.

Somebody in your family still has that console

That's the part that's almost always true. It didn't get thrown out. It got moved.

It's in a tub in a garage in Moore, or a closet in Edmond, or the corner of a spare room that nobody's fully unpacked since the last house. Along with the games, usually. Along with the sticky controller, definitely.

If that's your family's tub, it's worth more than anyone in the house assumes, and finding out takes one photo.

So: whose house was it at your family? Say the name in the comments — somebody's cousin is about to get called out twenty years late.
