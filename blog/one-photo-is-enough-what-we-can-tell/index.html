# One Photo Is Enough: What We Can Tell From a Picture

People send us apologies with their photos more often than they send us questions.

"Sorry, it's a mess." "Sorry, the light's bad." "Sorry, I didn't get everything out."

You genuinely don't need to apologise. A phone photo of an open bin, taken from where you're standing, tells us most of what we need to know — and this article is about exactly what that is, so you can stop worrying about the quality of the picture.

What we read in the first three seconds

Era. Console shapes, cartridge shells, packaging styles, the colour of the plastic — all of it dates a pile fast. We can usually place a photo within a console generation immediately, and that alone narrows the range enormously.

Volume. How much is actually there. A shoebox and a chest freezer full are very different conversations, and the number of things visible in one frame is a good proxy for the whole.

Condition, broadly. Not scratch-by-scratch — just the difference between "stored in a dry closet" and "stored in a garage that flooded once." Yellowed plastic, water staining, sun fade and mildew all read clearly even in a bad photo.

Whether the boxes survived. Boxed versus loose is one of the biggest single factors in what something is worth, and it's the easiest thing in the world to see in a photo.

What we'll ask a follow-up about

Sometimes one photo raises a specific question. When that happens we ask about that one thing rather than sending you off to catalogue the lot:

  • A close-up of one particular label or spine — usually because we think we recognise something uncommon and want to be sure.
  • The back or bottom of a console — model numbers and regional markings live there, and they occasionally matter a lot.
  • Whether something powers on — for anything where working-versus-not changes the number meaningfully. If you can't test it, say so; "don't know" is a completely acceptable answer and we price it accordingly.
  • What's under the top layer — if a bin looks deep, we might ask for a second photo halfway down.

That's it. Usually it's one follow-up, sometimes none.

What you don't need to do

Don't lay everything out. Grid photos look nice and take an hour. A pile in a bin takes four seconds and tells us the same things.

Don't clean anything. Dust doesn't change a number. Cleaning something the wrong way sometimes does.

Don't look up prices first. You're welcome to, but the numbers you'll find online are asking prices from people who may never sell at them, and completed-sale data is genuinely hard to read if you don't do it daily. It's the part of this we're actually for.

Don't separate out the "good stuff." The instinct is nearly always slightly wrong in both directions, and a mixed pile is easier for us to price than a curated one.

Don't worry about the light. Daylight through a window is plenty. Flash is fine. A dim garage photo is still a useful photo.

Then what?

You send the photo, we come back with a real cash number. If it works for you, we set a time and come to you — we do the lifting and the loading, and you get paid on the spot.

If it doesn't work for you, no hard feelings and no follow-up chasing. You'll know what it's worth, which is genuinely useful information to have either way.

The one-photo rule

The whole reason we work this way is that the alternative is what stops people. "Take an inventory, research it, sort it, then contact somebody" is a project. "Take a photo of the bin" is a thing you can do right now, from where you're sitting.

So take the photo. It's enough.
