# The Room They Left Behind

It's move-in weekend across the metro. The campuses are swallowing minivans by the hundred, the checkout lines are all shower caddies and twin-XL sheets, and by Sunday night a lot of houses around here are going to be one bedroom quieter.

Here's what nobody warns you about that room: the dorm takes surprisingly little of it. The clothes go. The laptop goes. Half the towels in the house mysteriously go. But the room — the room mostly stays. The shelf of games in the order they were last played. The tub of controllers. The figures on the dresser that have held the same formation since middle school, guarding the alarm clock like it's a checkpoint.

The museum period

Every family does the same thing with that room at first, and it's the right thing: nothing. The room becomes a small museum of about-sixteen-years-old, curated by whoever left it, visited mostly by the dog. There's no reason to touch it — Thanksgiving is coming, and winter break after that, and the person who owns all this will be back with laundry soon enough.

We'd genuinely encourage the museum period. There's no rush, and this post isn't here to invent one. The stuff has waited a decade; it can wait a semester.

The conversation worth having early, though

But somewhere between move-in and Thanksgiving, there's a ten-minute phone conversation worth having, and it's better had before anyone touches anything: "What in your room actually matters to you?"

The answers will surprise you in both directions. The complete console setup you assumed was sacred? "Oh, you can get rid of that, I haven't touched it since sophomore year." The one beat-up figure you'd have tossed with the happy-meal stuff? That one matters — that one goes to the dorm at Christmas. You cannot guess this inventory from the outside. The person who built the room holds the map, and they're never more reachable than in the first homesick month.

What that conversation buys you is clarity without conflict. The stuff that matters gets protected on purpose. The stuff that doesn't gets permission — and permission is the thing a parent can't grant themselves while standing alone in a kid's museum feeling weird about it.

When the room comes back

Some rooms stay museums for years and that's fine. But if the permission conversation happens and the answer is "honestly, most of it can go" — that's where we come in, at whatever speed suits. One photo of the shelf, the tub, the dresser formation, exactly as they sit. We'll tell you what we're seeing and come back with a real cash number. OKC metro, we pick up or meet, and the whole thing takes less time than the drive to campus did.

The room gets to become a guest room, an office, a gym that will briefly be a gym. And the good stuff doesn't get donated into the void — it goes to people who still light up about it.

No rush at all. But while it's fresh: what's still in your kid's old room? We've heard some impressive answers.
