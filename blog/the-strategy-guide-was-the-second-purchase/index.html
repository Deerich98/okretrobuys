The Strategy Guide Was The Second Purchase

August 18, 2026

# The Strategy Guide Was The Second Purchase

The game was the first purchase. The guide was the second — and there are people reading this who will quietly admit the guide was sometimes the better one.

Spiral-bound, so it could lie flat on the carpet next to the controller. Glossy pages that caught the lamp light. Maps drawn like national surveys, with every chest and every secret numbered. And the back sections — the bestiaries, the item tables, the appendices — written with the confidence of an encyclopedia about a world that didn't exist.

Read like literature

Nobody used the guide the way it was intended. The intended use was: get stuck, look it up, keep playing. The actual use was: read it cover to cover in bed, including — especially — the chapters for parts of the game you hadn't reached. Whole worlds got spoiled on purpose, happily, weeks in advance. You'd arrive at the final area with the terrain already memorized, like a pilgrim who'd studied the maps for years.

And then there were the guides for games you didn't even own. Everybody had one — bought in a bundle, gifted by a confused aunt, traded from a friend. You knew the item locations of a game you never played. Some of you still do. That knowledge is in there, permanent, taking up the shelf space in your head where a language could have gone.

The highlighter treatment

The truly devoted didn't just read; they studied. Highlighted like a textbook the week before finals. Sticky tabs on the boss chapters. Corners folded to the map pages, then folded flat again when the corner-folding felt disrespectful. A guide that got real use has a specific look — swollen at the spine, soft at the edges, a couple of pages that got wet once and dried wavy — and every mark in it is a record of a specific stuck afternoon, decades ago, when the answer was on page 141.

Why the shelf of them is worth a second look

Here's the practical footnote, from the buy-side of the counter: guides survived in weird numbers. They didn't get traded in with the games — the store didn't want them — so they went to closets, and they're still there, stacked flat under the board games. When a game collection comes our way, the guides riding along with it are part of the story: they mark which games got played to the bone, and complete sets — game, box, manual, and the guide somebody studied — are the collector's favorite version of the past.

So before a stack of old spiral-bound guides goes in the donation box with the magazines: that's not junk paper. That's the second purchase, and around here it still counts.
