The Catalogue You Circled

August 4, 2026

# The Catalogue You Circled

It arrived in the mail sometime in the autumn, and the whole house knew about it within the hour.

It was thick. It was glossy in the middle and newsprint at the edges. The front half was clothes and small appliances and nobody cared about any of it, and then somewhere past the middle the pages went full colour and there it was: the toy section. Twenty, thirty, sometimes fifty pages of it.

And you needed a pen.

The ritual

There was a proper method to it, and every kid's was slightly different.

Some people circled everything. Whole pages, ringed indiscriminately, a document of pure want with no strategy behind it at all.

Some people had a tier system — a firm circle for the real ask, a lighter circle for the acceptable substitute, and a small star next to the one thing they knew they weren't getting but wanted on the record anyway.

Some people wrote in the margins. Prices. Reasons. Notes addressed to a parent who was definitely going to read this, arguing the case in a child's handwriting.

And some people folded the corner of the page instead of circling, because a sibling was working the same copy and circles could be denied.

The catalogue was the negotiation document. It got left open on the kitchen table, page facing up, entirely by accident.

What you actually learned from it

Looking back, that catalogue was doing more than selling toys.

It taught you what things cost. You knew, at eight years old, that the big playset was $29.99 and the small one was $12.99 and there was a real difference between those two numbers in your household. That's a genuine early lesson in value, delivered by a toy company entirely by accident.

It taught you to want specifically. Not "a toy." That one, from that page, in that colour, with the accessory pack. The catalogue made you precise.

It gave you a season. The whole thing was time-bound. It arrived, you circled, the day came, and then it was over and the catalogue went in the recycling. There was no infinite scroll, no restock notification, no next drop. One shot a year, and then you waited.

The one you didn't get

Almost everybody has one.

Not because anyone was cruel about it — because it was too expensive, or it sold out, or it wasn't in stock at the store your parents actually went to, or because you got the cheaper version that was almost the same and you genuinely didn't mind at the time.

And then twenty-five or thirty years later you're on a phone at eleven at night looking at one, still in the box, and your finger is hovering over the button.

There's no shame in that at all. A lot of the collections we look at started with exactly one of those purchases.

Where they all ended up

Here's the part that connects the two ends of this. Most of those circled toys, the ones that did get bought, are still somewhere. Not on a shelf — in an attic bin, in a tub in a shed, in a closet at a parent's house, in a box that moved twice and never got opened.

Some of them are worth a surprising amount now. Some are worth exactly what you'd guess. The only way to know which is which is to look.

So: what did you circle? And be honest — did you ever get it, or did you buy it for yourself later? Both answers are extremely common around here.
